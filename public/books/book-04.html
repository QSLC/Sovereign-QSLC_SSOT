Sample — Chapter 1: Why Businesses Break

Meridian's first outside prospect did not ask for artificial intelligence. The owner asked why a completed job took so long to become an invoice. Work passed from a technician to a supervisor, then to administration, then back to the technician for missing information. Each person was busy. The delay belonged to the spaces between their responsibilities.

Businesses often break at handoffs. A department optimizes its own activity while the customer experiences the entire journey. The technician measures completed work. Administration measures processed invoices. Nobody measures the age of completed work waiting for sufficient evidence. Automation added to one department can accelerate the delivery of incomplete material to the next.

Meridian resisted the urge to demonstrate its dashboard. The team followed one job from customer request to payment eligibility. They observed the actual sequence, the information required at each transition, the exceptions, and the people empowered to resolve them. The product opportunity appeared as a specific broken promise: accepted work should reach billing with the evidence necessary to invoice it.

The diagnostic framework was a handoff examination. Identify the sender, receiver, entry criteria, required evidence, completion signal, and exception owner. Then measure waiting time separately from processing time. A five-minute task can take ten days when it waits in four unowned queues. The proposed solution must address the delay's cause rather than decorate its visibility.

This diagnosis also prevents selling the wrong scale of product. A small organization with one broken handoff may need a modest workflow and review routine. A multi-location enterprise may need integration, identity controls, and governance. A sophisticated platform can be economically inappropriate even when technically impressive.

Commercial exercise. Interview an authorized buyer about one recurring operational failure. Ask for a recent example, the affected person, the sequence of handoffs, and the consequence. Draw the workflow in the buyer's own language. Propose no features until the buyer confirms that the map describes the problem. End with one measurable outcome the buyer would recognize as improvement.

The protocol economy begins at a break somebody cares enough to repair. Meridian's first commercial discipline was learning to hear the problem before naming the product.
