Sample — Chapter 1: Duplicate Reality

At Meridian Works, the argument began with a delivery date. Sales had told the customer Friday. The production board showed Monday. A spreadsheet copied into a planning meeting showed Thursday. None of the people holding these dates considered themselves dishonest. Each could explain where the date had come from. The organization had created three plausible histories and neglected to appoint a way to reconcile them.

A duplicate record is more dangerous than a duplicate file. Files can contain the same bytes and still serve different purposes. Records become competing realities when two places claim authority over the same entity and attribute. The customer number may match while the promised date, payment status, or approval state differs. Without an explicit rule, the system often chooses whichever value it encounters most recently or whichever person has the most influence.

Begin with identity. A customer needs a durable identifier that survives spelling changes and mergers. An order needs an identifier separate from the customer. A promise needs a source, time, and person authorized to make it. When those distinctions disappear, a delivery date can drift from a sales estimate into a contractual commitment merely because someone copied a column.

Meridian's first repair was a reality register. For every disputed attribute, the team recorded the entity identifier, competing values, original sources, meaning, and responsible decision maker. They discovered that Thursday meant planned production completion, Friday meant proposed delivery, and Monday meant the carrier's next available service. The apparent contradiction partly reflected different events. The remaining disagreement required a customer conversation and an approved change.

The framework is an identity-and-meaning test. Ask whether two statements concern the same entity, the same attribute, the same time period, and the same definition. Only after those questions are answered should the organization merge or reject a value. Automatic deduplication before semantic comparison can erase a legitimate distinction.

EVE can help detect competing dates and assemble the evidence. It should not quietly choose a convenient answer. Its useful output is a discrepancy object: what differs, why the difference matters, what evidence is available, and who can resolve it. This turns a confusing conversation into bounded work.

Field exercise. Select one customer, one order, and one operational date. Find every place that date appears. Label the event each occurrence describes. Record one authoritative owner and a resolution rule. Finish by asking a colleague to reconstruct the agreed answer without your help. If the colleague needs your memory, the repair is incomplete.

The city's first law was modest: two convincing documents could disagree, and confidence alone would not decide between them.
